On finishing things

Eleven half-built projects in a folder called “later.” An essay about why “later” is the most expensive word in the English language.

A
Anya Petrov
Apr 12 · Member since 2022

There’s a folder on my laptop called later. It contains eleven directories, each one a project I started in earnest, made meaningful progress on, and then quietly abandoned at the moment it began to ask something difficult of me.

For years I told myself I was a person who started things. This was true, and I wore it like a small medal. What I wasn’t telling myself is that starting is the easy part. The first eight hours of any project are mostly play — assembling, naming, choosing fonts, sketching diagrams that won’t survive contact with the real problem.

Finishing is the part where the project starts asking who you actually are.

Finishing is the part where the project stops being a fun rearrangement of possibility and starts asking who you actually are. It demands that you commit to one path while the others go dark. It requires you to be wrong, in public, with detail.

So we don’t finish. We pivot, we “take a break,” we spin up something new because newness pretends to be progress. Meanwhile the half-built thing sits in later, accruing the kind of debt that compounds inside you rather than on a balance sheet.

The most useful skill I’ve learned in the last decade isn’t a framework or a language. It’s the small, daily, unglamorous act of returning. Of opening the file again. Of sitting with the boring middle of the work — the part with no novelty, no applause, just the quiet conviction that you said you would.
