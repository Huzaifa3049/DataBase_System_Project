On finishing things

A short essay about the most expensive word in the English language: later.

T
Theo Vance
Apr 22, 2026 · 8 min read · 4,201 followers

I have eleven half-built projects in a folder called later. A reading app I started in 2022. Three essays I outlined and then abandoned. A garden plan. A novel that has the same first chapter four different ways.

Each of them was, at the moment of beginning, the most exciting thing I'd ever made. And each of them, at some point, became less interesting than the next idea — and so I shelved it, with the quiet promise that I'd come back.

The cost of later

Here is what I've learned about that promise. Later is not a delay. It's a discount. Every time I move something into the later folder, I am quietly telling myself that it doesn't have to be good — because someday, when I have more time and skill and clarity, I'll come back and finish it properly.

The work is never finished by a future, more competent version of you. It is finished by you, today, in your current state of partial knowledge and fading interest.

This year, I made a rule. I'm allowed to start something only if I'm willing to finish the worst version of it. Not the best version. The worst. The version that exists at the end of an afternoon, badly, but completely.

[ illustration: photograph of a desk at dusk ]

It turns out that finishing the worst version of a thing is its own skill. It's a different muscle than the one that gets you started. It requires you to ignore the part of your brain that whispers but it's not good yet, and to ship it anyway. To call it done. To close the file.

The eleven half-projects are still in my later folder. I'm not going to finish them. That's the trade. To finish things, you have to be willing to never finish other things — and that, more than anything, is the part nobody tells you.
